## Executive Summary

The bond market represents one of the most systemically important yet intimidating markets for macro investors. Despite being filled with complex jargon and technicalities, bond markets can be thoroughly understood through a comprehensive two-fold analytical framework. This foundational lesson introduces both the macro approach—which examines bond yields through growth expectations, inflation expectations, and term premium—and the technical approach—which views bond yields as strips of future overnight interest rates discounted to present value. Together, these complementary approaches provide a complete understanding of how bond markets function and what drives yield movements across different time horizons.
## Introduction: Demystifying the Bond Market

The bond market is large and systemically important for macro investors, yet it scares away plenty of people due to its complexity. The market is full of jargon and technicalities, which creates barriers to understanding for many investors. This comprehensive course aims to break down these complexities, make the content jargon-free, and ensure that students can master the bond market together through a structured, methodical approach.

The foundation for truly understanding bond markets lies in employing a twofold approach that combines both macro and technical perspectives. This dual framework allows investors to analyze bond yields from multiple angles, ensuring a complete understanding of the forces that drive bond market movements.

## Approach #1: The Macro Framework

### The Fundamental Equation

The macro approach views bond yields through a fundamental equation that breaks down the components driving yield levels:

**Bond Yield = Growth Expectations + Inflation Expectations + Term Premium**

This equation serves as the cornerstone for understanding long-term bond yield movements and provides insight into why yields behave as they do across different economic environments.

### Growth Expectations: The Critical Distinction Between Cyclical and Structural Growth

When examining growth expectations within the bond yield framework, it's essential to understand the critical distinction between cyclical growth and structural growth, as each affects different segments of the bond market in distinct ways.

#### Structural Growth: The Long-End Driver

For the long end of the bond market, structural growth is what matters most. Structural growth represents an economy's ability to generate potential growth over extended periods and is primarily driven by two fundamental factors:

1. **Demographics**: The composition and trends in working-age population
2. **Productivity**: The efficiency with which an economy can produce goods and services

Structural growth provides the foundation for long-term economic expansion and directly influences investor expectations for long-dated bond yields. When structural growth prospects are strong, investors typically demand higher yields to compensate for the opportunity cost of holding bonds instead of growth-oriented investments.

#### Cyclical Growth: The Short-End Influence

Cyclical growth, in contrast, is more important for the short end of the bond market. There are cycles in which economic growth is above or below potential structural growth, and these fluctuations are driven by several key factors:

- **Fiscal stance**: Whether government policy is stimulative or restrictive
- **Credit availability**: Whether credit eligibility for the private sector is abundant or tight
- **Earnings cycle and labor market conditions**: Whether these are growing healthily or experiencing stress

The economic cycle matters more for the short end of the bond curve than it does for the long end because short-term rates are more directly influenced by central bank policy responses to cyclical conditions.

### Historical Analysis: The Demographic Challenge

#### Working-Age Population Trends

The demographic component of structural growth tells a compelling story about the challenges facing developed economies. The working-age population as a percentage of total population serves as a crucial metric for understanding how demographics impact economic growth potential.

Historical data reveals that many European jurisdictions—including France, Italy, Germany, and Britain—along with Japan and the United States, all experienced very positive demographic trends between the 1960s and early 1990s. This positive trend resulted from a demographic boom following the Second World War that helped expand the labor force across these jurisdictions.

During this period, the percentage of the total population that was working and actively contributing to economic growth was expanding, which significantly helped the structural growth prospects of these economies. A larger working-age population means more people contributing to economic output, innovation, and productivity.

However, from the 1990s onwards, the exact opposite trend emerged. Working-age population as a percentage of total population peaked in many jurisdictions and began inexorably declining. Japan led this demographic transition with an aging population and poor fertility rates, but all other major developed jurisdictions have followed Japan down this challenging demographic path over the last 20 years.

World Bank projections for the next 30 years indicate that this situation is unlikely to improve significantly. This demographic reality means that as economies experience a lower percentage of their population actively working, their ability to generate structural growth—which is driven by demographics—becomes constrained.

#### The Productivity Puzzle

The second major driver of structural growth, productivity, has also presented challenges over recent decades. In the United States, total factor productivity (TFP) growth averaged approximately 1% over the last three decades between the 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s. This represents relatively flat productivity growth that has not increased meaningfully over this extended period.

More concerning, the trend in productivity growth over the last few decades has actually been declining rather than improving. This stagnant productivity performance means that the second main component behind structural growth was unable to offset the decline in working-age population and the deterioration in demographics.

Combining these two factors—declining demographics and stagnant productivity—effectively means that structural growth expectations have been trending down aggressively over the past several decades. This deterioration in structural growth fundamentals represents one of the primary reasons why long-dated bond yields have been declining relentlessly over the last 30 years.

### Inflation Expectations: The Long-Term Perspective

#### The Evolution of Inflation Expectations

When discussing inflation expectations within the bond yield framework, it's crucial to understand that the focus is not on today's Consumer Price Index (CPI) or current inflation readings. Instead, the emphasis is on long-term expectations about inflation—similar to the distinction made between cyclical and structural growth.

For long-dated bond yields, what matters is not today's inflation but rather long-term expectations about where inflation will settle over extended periods. The short end of the bond market is more influenced by current inflation readings because these directly impact central bank decision-making in the near term.

#### Historical Inflation Expectation Trends

The University of Michigan Survey of 5-10 year ahead inflation expectations provides valuable insight into how long-term inflation expectations have evolved over time. This data reveals that inflation expectations were very elevated in the 1980s, reaching approximately 8%. These expectations remained around 4-5% through the late 1980s.

From the 1990s onwards, however, the trend in long-term inflation expectations was predominantly declining, eventually stabilizing around 2.5-3%. This declining trend in inflation expectations paralleled the deterioration in structural growth expectations, creating a dual headwind for bond yields.

Core inflation data followed essentially the same declining path as inflation expectations. This consistency between expectations and actual outcomes validates the declining trend, with actual inflation barely averaging 2% over the last two decades.

#### The China Factor and Disinflationary Forces

One of the main drivers behind declining inflation expectations—though there are many factors that could be covered—was the significant and rapid increase in Chinese working-age population between the 1970s and 2000s.

The rapid expansion of China's working-age population during the 1990s, growing from around 400 million people to almost one billion by 2020 (more than doubling), created a massive supply of cheap labor available to Western economies. China's abundant workforce enabled Western companies to offshore manufacturing and production more aggressively, driving down labor costs and creating a powerful disinflationary force for Western economies (Direct Cost Reduction: When companies move production to countries with cheaper labor, their production costs drop significantly. A product that might cost $20 to manufacture domestically could cost $5 to produce in China due to lower wages, fewer regulations, and economies of scale).

Beyond the China effect, additional disinflationary forces included online sales, digitalization, and technological advancement that helped reduce labor costs. A striking statistic illustrates this transformation: in the 1980s, for a US company to generate the equivalent of one million in sales (in today's terms), it needed approximately seven to eight times the number of employees required today.

This increased reliance on capital, digitalization, and technology rather than labor created strong disinflationary pressures that contributed to declining long-term inflation expectations and persistently low actual inflation readings (When you replace workers with machines or software, your cost per additional unit produced drops dramatically. A factory robot might cost $100,000 upfront but can work 24/7 for years without wages, benefits, or breaks. Once you've made that capital investment, producing each additional unit becomes much cheaper. This and other reasons like Fixed vs. Variable Cost Structure, Productivity Multiplication (through digital tools), Competitive Pressure to Lower Prices and more is why periods of rapid technological advancement often coincide with low inflation or even deflation).

#### Future Implications

United Nations projections for Chinese working-age population over the next 20-30 years show a reversal of the previous trend. China is projected to experience a shrinking working-age population, meaning the supply of cheap labor to global economies will not continue to provide the same disinflationary force it has for the past 30 years.

### Term Premium: Compensation for Interest Rate Risk

#### Defining Term Premium

The third component of the macro approach is term premium, which can be understood as the compensation that investors demand to take on interest rate risk rather than simply choosing to own and roll Treasury bills repeatedly.

When investors consider fixed income investments, they face a choice between two strategies:

1. **Rolling strategy**: Buying Treasury bills and rolling them over for an extended period (e.g., 10 years)
2. **Duration strategy**: Buying 10-year bonds today and locking in yields for the entire 10-year period

The duration strategy requires investors to take on interest rate risk. If uncertainty about growth expectations and inflation expectations over the next 10 years is wide, investors will require a larger term premium—greater compensation—to take on that interest rate risk today rather than maintaining the flexibility of rolling three-month Treasury bills.

#### The Relationship Between Uncertainty and Term Premium

The level of term premium is inversely related to the predictability of future economic conditions. When the uncertainty is lower and the path around growth and inflation over the next 10 years is more predictable, the term premium will be lower. Conversely, higher uncertainty requires higher compensation for taking on interest rate risk.

### The Leverage Response: Economic Policy Implications

#### The Policy Challenge

Politicians and society observed structural growth and structural inflation expectations declining relentlessly for 20-30 years. When an economy cannot generate politically and socially acceptable levels of nominal GDP growth through structural means, policymakers must find alternative ways to boost cyclical growth.

This challenge led to a systematic approach of overlaying cyclical growth stimulus on top of poor structural growth and inflation expectation trends. The primary tools for achieving this cyclical boost are:

- **Fiscal stimulus**: Government spending and tax policies to stimulate economic activity
- **Abundant and cheap credit**: Making borrowing easier and less expensive for both businesses and consumers

#### The Debt Accumulation Pattern

The response to declining structural fundamentals was extensive leverage across developed economies. In the United States, total private and public debt as a percentage of GDP increased dramatically from approximately 180% in the 1990s to almost 300% immediately following the pandemic.

Different jurisdictions chose varying approaches to this leverage strategy:

- **Public debt focus**: Countries like Italy and Japan primarily used government balance sheets to add leverage
- **Private debt focus**: Countries like Canada, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Australia, Korea, Norway, and China avoided large public debt increases but accumulated substantial private sector debt

Despite appearing more "virtuous" in terms of public debt levels, countries in the second category created mountains of private leverage. When examining total economic leverage across all developed market jurisdictions, the same fundamental trend emerges: economies systematically levered up to replace dwindling nominal growth with cyclical growth through fiscal deficits and abundant cheap credit.

#### The Yield Impact of Increased Leverage

This leverage accumulation directly impacted bond yields through a fundamental economic relationship: more debt equals lower yields. US 10-year Treasury yields over the last 30 years effectively mirrored the trend of total debt to GDP.

This creates a self-reinforcing cycle that's easy to understand. As an economy takes on more debt (levers up)—whether through businesses borrowing to expand, consumers financing purchases, or governments funding spending—the entire system becomes dependent on that borrowed money.

Take a simple example: when a developer gets a $10 million loan to build condos, the bank doesn't transfer existing money from other accounts. Instead, they simultaneously create $10 million in new debt (the developer's obligation to repay) and $10 million in new spending power that didn't exist before. This process (debt and credit creation) literally expands the money supply every time someone borrows. 

Debt Created:

- The developer now owes the bank $10 million (plus interest)
- This is a brand new debt obligation that didn't exist before
- The developer created a promise to pay this money back over time

Credit Created:

- The bank deposits $10 million into the developer's account
- This $10 million is new spending power that can be used immediately
- This money didn't exist anywhere in the economy before the loan was made

But here's the catch: to keep this debt-fueled growth machine (highly leveraged system) running, central banks must continuously lower interest rates. If rates were 8% instead of 2%, that developer couldn't afford the loan payments, the condos wouldn't get built, construction workers lose jobs, and the economy contracts. Higher rates would make debt payments unaffordable across the board, potentially triggering widespread defaults and economic collapse.

So the more debt an economy accumulates, the more it needs cheap money to survive—creating an addiction to low interest rates.

The equilibrium interest rates for a highly leveraged economy are necessarily lower because maintaining the stability of such a system requires lower borrowing costs. This dynamic became one of the major drivers behind consistently lower bond yields over the past three decades.

#### Term Premium Compression

The leverage-driven model also contributed to lower term premium over time. The predictability around the future path for growth expectations, inflation expectations, and interest rates became very high under this framework.

The economic model became clear and predictable: continuously lever up the economy to offset structural declines in growth expectations and structural disinflationary forces. The more leverage added to the economy, the more likely it became that interest rates would need to remain low to maintain system stability.

This high predictability reduced the compensation investors required to take on interest rate risk. The uncertainty around the future direction of bond yields was minimal—they were clearly heading lower—so term premium compression was a natural outcome of this macroeconomic framework.

## Approach #2: The Technical Framework

### The Limitations of Macro-Only Analysis

While the macro approach provides essential insights into long-term bond yield trends, it has limitations that require a complementary analytical framework. The 1940s provide a compelling example of when the macro approach alone would have been insufficient for understanding bond market behavior.

#### The 1940s Example: When Macro Fundamentals Diverged from Market Reality

During the 1940s, one of the main drivers from the macro approach—consumer price inflation and inflation expectations—was rising rapidly to levels of 10%, 15%, or even 20%. These were extremely elevated levels of inflation and inflation expectations.

Despite having one of the main macro drivers pointing toward higher interest rates and higher yields, nominal interest rates were actually as negative as -10% to -15% in real terms. Nominal interest rates remained stuck near zero throughout this period.

This disconnect occurred because of central bank dominance and yield curve control. The Federal Reserve was effectively pinning long-dated yields at 0% despite macro fundamentals pointing to much higher appropriate yield levels. This example demonstrates that sometimes central bank policy can override macro fundamentals for extended periods.

### Understanding Bonds Through Cash Flows

#### The Fundamental Bond Pricing Formula

The technical approach introduces a different way of thinking about bond yields through cash flow analysis. The basic bond pricing formula provides the foundation for this approach:

**PB = C₁/(1+i)¹ + C₂/(1+i)² + ... + (Cₙ + Fₙ)/(1+i)ᴺ**

Where:

- PB = Price of the bond
- C = Coupon payments
- F = Face value
- i = Discount rate
- N = Number of periods

This formula shows that bond prices represent the present value of all future cash flows, including both coupon payments and the final principal repayment, all discounted back to time zero using appropriate discount factors.

#### From Bonds to Swaps: A More Powerful Framework

Instead of thinking only about bond coupons, the introduction of swaps provides a more sophisticated framework for understanding interest rate relationships. Through swaps, 10-year interest rates can be conceptualized as a strip of all future Fed funds overnight rates for the next 10 years, discounted back to today.

This means that the 10-year yield displayed on financial screens every day can be understood as nothing more than the equivalent present value of all the overnight Fed funds rates that will prevail every day for the next 10 years.

### Swaps: The Mechanics of Interest Rate Relationships

#### What Are Swaps?

Swaps are contractual agreements between two counterparties to exchange one set of cash flows for another. Specifically, interest rate swaps involve exchanging:

- **Fixed leg**: A set of fixed cash flows determined by a fixed interest rate
- **Floating leg**: A set of floating cash flows determined by variable future interest rates

#### Swap Pricing Fundamentals

The mathematical framework for swap pricing includes several key components:

**Present Value Formulations:**

- PV = present value
- N = notional amount
- rFixed = fixed rate
- P(t,T) = discount factor
- Lⱼ = floating rate at date j
- S = spread

Most swaps are entered with a present value of zero at inception, meaning the present value of the fixed leg equals the present value of the floating leg. This equality is achieved by setting:

**∑ NrFixed τᵢP(tₑ,tᵢ) = ∑ N(Lⱼ₋₁ + s)τⱼP(tₑ,tⱼ)**

#### The Key Insight: Interest Rates as Strips of Future Overnight Rates

This swap pricing mechanism reveals a fundamental insight: by setting the difference between the two legs to zero, the fixed interest rate (such as a 10-year Treasury yield) becomes mathematically equivalent to all future overnight floating rates for the next 10 years, discounted back to time zero.

This relationship allows investors and analysts to think of any fixed-rate bond yield as representing the market's current expectation of what overnight interest rates will average over the corresponding time period.

### Practical Application: Pricing a 5-Year Overnight Index Swap

#### Swap Structure and Current Market Conditions

To illustrate these concepts practically, consider pricing a 5-year overnight index swap with the following characteristics:

**Counterparty Roles:**

- **Counterparty A**: Pays 5-year fixed interest rate (3.7875%) and receives floating overnight rate
- **Counterparty B**: Pays floating overnight rate for 5 years and receives 3.7875% fixed

This represents an agreement to exchange fixed cash flows for floating cash flows, with the present values designed to be equal at inception.

#### Market Expectations for Fed Funds

Current market pricing for overnight Fed funds over the next five years shows:

- **2024 and early 2025**: Fed funds priced at approximately 5%
- **2025-2028**: Gradual decline to approximately 3%

#### Cash Flow Analysis by Period

**Early Period (2024-Early 2025):** During this period, the counterparty B (paying floating) loses money because:

- Receives: 3.7875% fixed rate
- Pays: ~5% floating rate (higher than received)
- Net result: Negative cash flow

**Later Period (2026-2028):** During this period, the counterparty B makes money because:

- Receives: 3.7875% fixed rate
- Pays: ~3% floating rate (lower than received, as Fed is priced to cut rates)
- Net result: Positive cash flow

#### The Present Value Equilibrium

When all these cash flows are summed and discounted to present value, they equal zero. This mathematical equality demonstrates that the 3.7875% fixed 5-year interest rate represents exactly the same economic value as the sum of all future floating Fed funds rates from 2025 through 2028, properly discounted to today.

**Key Principle**: The fixed 5-year yield displayed on screens every day is mathematically equivalent to the present value of all expected future overnight interest rates for the next five years.

### Applying the Technical Approach to Historical Examples

#### Revisiting the 1940s with Technical Analysis

Using the technical approach to analyze the 1940s provides clarity that the macro approach alone could not deliver. While macro fundamentals suggested that inflation expectations in double digits and high uncertainty (requiring elevated term premium) should have produced much higher bond yields, the technical approach offers a different perspective.

If the Federal Reserve was determined to keep Fed funds at zero for a prolonged period through yield curve control, then thinking of 10-year yields as a strip of all future Fed funds for the next 10 years makes the zero nominal yield environment completely understandable.

When the central bank commits to keeping overnight rates at zero for an extended period, the technical approach predicts that longer-term yields will also remain near zero, regardless of what macro fundamentals might suggest as appropriate levels.

#### The Power of Combined Analysis

This example illustrates why combining both approaches creates a more powerful analytical framework. The macro approach provides insight into fundamental economic forces and long-term trends, while the technical approach reveals how central bank policy and market mechanics can override these fundamentals for significant periods.

## Integration and Future Application

### Synthesis of the Two-Fold Approach

The comprehensive understanding of bond markets emerges from skillfully combining both the macro and technical approaches:

**Macro Approach Strengths:**

- Explains long-term secular trends in bond yields
- Identifies fundamental drivers of growth and inflation expectations
- Provides insight into structural economic changes
- Helps understand term premium dynamics

**Technical Approach Strengths:**

- Explains short-term yield movements driven by policy expectations
- Provides precision in understanding central bank influence
- Offers mathematical framework for yield relationships
- Enables analysis during periods of central bank dominance

### Key Takeaways for Practical Application

**For Long-Term Analysis:**

- Focus primarily on structural growth fundamentals (demographics and productivity)
- Monitor long-term inflation expectations rather than current CPI readings
- Assess term premium through uncertainty about future economic conditions
- Consider total economy leverage as a key driver of equilibrium yield levels

**For Short-Term Analysis:**

- Use the technical approach to understand central bank influence
- Think of yields as present values of expected future overnight rates
- Monitor swap markets for insights into policy expectations
- Consider central bank communication and policy stance

**For Risk Management:**

- Understand that macro and technical factors can diverge significantly
- Prepare for periods when central bank policy overrides fundamentals
- Monitor both approaches continuously for comprehensive market understanding
- Use the framework to identify potential turning points in bond market cycles

## Conclusion

Mastering the bond market requires abandoning the notion that it's "all about QE" and central banks alone. While central bank policy certainly matters, the comprehensive two-fold approach reveals that bond markets are driven by a complex interaction of structural economic forces, policy responses, and market mechanics.

The macro approach provides the fundamental framework for understanding why bond yields have followed their long-term trajectory, emphasizing the critical roles of demographics, productivity, inflation expectations, and the policy response to structural economic challenges. The technical approach offers precision in understanding how these fundamental forces translate into actual market pricing, particularly during periods when central bank policy may temporarily override fundamental considerations.

Together, these approaches create a powerful analytical framework that enables investors, analysts, and policymakers to understand bond market behavior across different time horizons and market conditions.
